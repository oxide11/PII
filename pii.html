The Science of Re-identification

Why does combining seemingly safe data (like a ZIP code and Gender) trigger a strict PI violation? The answer lies in Linkage Attacks and the mathematical concept of k-anonymity.

The Linkage Attack Mechanism

Data is rarely breached in a vacuum. Hackers re-identify "anonymous" tables by overlaying them with public datasets.

Anonymous Health Data

The 87% Rule (The Mosaic Effect)

You might think thousands of people share your ZIP code and birthday. Mathematically, they do not. Privacy scientists proved that the combination of just three demographic traits acts as a unique fingerprint for the vast majority of the population.

Academic Citation:
"It was found that 87% (216 million of 248 million) of the population in the United States had reported characteristics that likely made them unique based only on [5-digit ZIP, gender, date of birth]."

— Sweeney, L. (2000). Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely. Carnegie Mellon University, Data Privacy Working Paper 3.

The minimum group size you want for every combination of quasi-identifiers. Higher k = stronger privacy target = more aggressive flagging. k=5 is typical moderate privacy; k=10 is strong; k=25 is medical / research-grade.
Average information content per quasi-identifier in bits. ~11 matches Sweeney's demographic triple (5-digit ZIP + full DOB + gender). Lower values model coarser fields (state, birth year). Higher values model narrower fields (9-digit ZIP, employer name).
How the math works
k-anonymity requires each combination of quasi-identifiers to represent at least k people. With N rows and ~b bits per quasi, the maximum number of quasi-identifiers before a combination becomes rarer than k is Qsafe = ⌊log₂(N/k) / b⌋. The Mosaic threshold is Qsafe + 1 — the first Q at which the schema likely leaks — clamped to at least 2 so a single quasi cannot flip the banner by itself. CCPA adds +1 to this threshold because of its narrower de-identification standard.
